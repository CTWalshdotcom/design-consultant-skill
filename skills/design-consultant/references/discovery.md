# Taste discovery

How to find what Conor likes when he cannot name it. He reacts; you translate. Run the exercises in this order, one at a time, and skip any the taste profile already answers for this kind of asset.

Sources behind these methods: style tiles (styletil.es; A List Apart, "Style Tiles and How They Work"), element collages (Dan Mall; Cloud Four, "Element collages are fun"), product reaction cards (Microsoft Desirability Toolkit; MeasuringU), semantic-differential sliders and checking that adjectives mean the same to both sides (Quirks, "Taking a qual-quant approach to design research"; Hike One brand workshops), image preference tests (Ballpark; 18F visual preference testing), asking about dislikes (Cyncly), and Authority Hacker's Vibe Code Websites V2 lessons 3 to 7. These measure preference, not conversion: conversion is judged separately in step 9.

## 1. Pick words

Show the list as options (several selects). Ask for up to five it should feel like, then up to five it must never feel like.

Calm, Bold, Playful, Serious, Warm, Cool, Friendly, Authoritative, Premium, Approachable, Simple, Rich, Quiet, Loud, Modern, Classic, Technical, Human, Precise, Relaxed, Energetic, Trustworthy, Surprising, Expected, Crafted, Efficient, Raw, Polished, Spacious, Dense, Honest, Clever.

Then check meaning: for each picked word, show one small picture of what you take it to mean on this asset (the same content, styled to the word) and ask "is this what you mean by [word]?". A word he means differently goes into the glossary with his meaning.

## 2. This or that

Six to ten pairs. Each pair is the same content, at the same size and quality, with **one** thing changed. Build them from his real content (his headline, his chart, his slide), never lorem or stock. Ask A, B or neither; offer an optional "why".

Pick the pairs the brief has not settled, from:

- spacious or dense
- big type or small type
- one strong colour or quiet colour throughout
- photos or drawings (or no pictures)
- square corners or rounded corners
- left-aligned or centred
- flat shapes or outlined shapes
- one column or several
- serious type or friendly type
- motion that is still or motion that moves (show as two short clips or a before and after frame)
- light page or dark page
- tight, compact controls or large, roomy controls

For dashboards and reports add: chart-first or numbers-first; one big answer or a grid of equal tiles; colour for meaning only or colour for brand.

## 3. Sliders

Five scales, each answered as a position from 1 to 7 with a picture at each end:

- friendly to authoritative
- playful to serious
- simple to rich
- classic to fresh
- calm to energetic

## 4. Examples he likes and hates

Ask for two to four things he likes, from anywhere (a website, an app, packaging, a poster, a shop), and one or two he hates. Take screenshots or links he gives; never trawl for them. For each, say in plain words what you think he is reacting to (the space, the colour, the type, the pictures, the buttons, the personality), and ask him to confirm. When he shares references, list every feature they share (colour blocks, rules, icons, header style, how contact is set out) and build each one in by default; he strikes what he does not want. Research rules or a design system never drop a feature silently. Never ask him to pick from a short list of a reference's features: show the full list as kept and ask what to remove.

## 5. The translation

Write his taste back as short lines, plain words first and the designer's term in brackets:

- "lots of room around things (white space)"
- "one strong colour, used only on the main button (a single accent)"
- "square corners (no border radius)"
- "big, heavy headline over small plain text (strong type contrast)"

He corrects any line. The corrected list goes into the brief, and anything that holds across projects goes into the taste profile.

## Glossary to grow

Keep a running glossary in the taste profile: each designer term used, its plain meaning, and a picture reference when one was shown. Over time he gains the words; the skill never makes him use them.
